This change swaps declining-balance interest for flat interest on the original principal. That is not a simplification, it is a different financial product.

**What breaks:**

1. **Borrowers are overcharged.** On a declining-balance loan, interest falls each period as the outstanding principal is paid down. Computing interest on `disbursed` every period charges interest on principal the borrower has already repaid. Over the life of the loan this is a large overcharge, and on most consumer-lending regimes it misstates the APR/truth-in-lending disclosure.

2. **The schedule no longer amortises to zero.** The `emi` was derived for a declining-balance schedule. Holding interest constant at the period-one level keeps `principal = emi − interest` too small every period, so `outstanding` never reaches zero at term end. There is a residual balance the code silently ignores.

3. **The passing tests prove nothing.** The comment admits the fixture is flat-rate, the one case where declining-balance and flat interest coincide. Green tests here are evidence the tests don't cover the behaviour being changed, not evidence the change is safe.

The comment frames this as "simpler, constant" — it is, but simplicity of the arithmetic isn't the goal; the loan's economics are. Nothing in the diff suggests the product was intended to move from reducing-balance to flat-rate, and such a move would need product, pricing and compliance sign-off, not a one-line refactor justified by a passing flat-rate fixture.

DECISION: BLOCK
REASON: The change replaces declining-balance interest with flat interest on the original principal, overcharging borrowers and leaving a non-zero residual balance at term end, and the passing tests only coincide because the fixture is flat-rate.
